Getting a PCOS diagnosis: what to expect

Blood tests, scans and a whole lot of questions. Here's how a PCOS diagnosis usually works on the NHS and how to make your appointment count.

Published 6 October 2026 Last updated 2 November 2026 2 min read

Sample article — this is placeholder content to show the layout. Delete or replace it before launch.

Think you might have PCOS? Getting a diagnosis can feel like a maze, but knowing the route makes it much less daunting. Here's the usual path.

Step one: talking to your GP

Your GP will ask about your periods, your skin, hair growth, weight changes and any family history. This bit really matters, so don't downplay anything. To help, try to bring:

  • Your period dates for the last few months (a tracking app is great for this)
  • A list of symptoms, including ones you're not sure are related
  • Any family history of PCOS or type 2 diabetes
  • A note of what you want – answers, symptom relief, or just reassurance

Step two: tests

Your GP may arrange blood tests to check your hormone levels and rule out other conditions that can cause similar symptoms, like thyroid problems. Some tests may need to be done at a particular point in your cycle.

You might also be offered an ultrasound scan to look at your ovaries. For younger people, doctors often rely more on symptoms and blood tests, because ovaries naturally look a bit "polycystic" in the years after periods start.

Step three: the diagnosis

PCOS is usually diagnosed when you have at least two of the three key signs: irregular periods, signs of higher androgens, and polycystic ovaries on a scan, once other causes have been ruled out. You might be referred to a specialist if things aren't clear-cut.

If you feel brushed off

It's frustratingly common to be told to "lose weight" or "come back if it gets worse". You're allowed to ask what tests have been done, ask for a second opinion, or see a different GP at your practice. Advocating for yourself isn't being difficult. It's being smart.

When to see your GP

If your periods are irregular or missing, or you've got symptoms that are affecting your day-to-day life, book that appointment. If you're unsure whether something is urgent, NHS 111 can help.
