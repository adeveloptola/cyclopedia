Symptom tracking that doctors actually find useful

A good symptom diary can speed up a diagnosis. Here's what to track, how to track it, and how to share it with your GP.

Published 29 October 2026 Last updated 29 October 2026 2 min read

Sample article — this is placeholder content to show the layout. Delete or replace it before launch.

"How long has this been going on?" "How bad is it, out of ten?" If those questions leave you guessing, a symptom diary is your new best mate. It turns "it's been rubbish for ages" into evidence a GP can actually work with.

What to track

You don't need to log everything. Focus on the stuff that helps paint a picture:

  • Cycle dates: first day of each period and how many days it lasts
  • Flow: how often you change pads, tampons or cups, and any flooding or large clots
  • Pain: where it is, how bad (0–10), and when it happens — before, during or between periods, or during sex or going to the loo
  • Mood: low, anxious, irritable or okay, especially in the days before your period
  • Impact: days off school or work, plans cancelled, sleep lost
  • What you tried: painkillers, heat, the pill, and whether they helped

Try to keep it going for at least two or three cycles. Patterns over time are what make doctors sit up.

Keep it simple

The best tracker is the one you'll actually use. A period app, the notes on your phone or a paper diary all work. A quick daily score takes thirty seconds and is better than a detailed log you give up on after a week.

Sharing it with your GP

Don't hand over three months of raw notes and hope for the best. Write a short summary on top: "Periods every 26 days, lasting 8 days, heavy for 3. Pain 8/10 on days 1–2, off school twice a month." Then bring the full diary in case they want the detail.

Your data is your voice in black and white. Start tracking today, and walk into that appointment with the receipts.
